20 note alert
A solstice fact (1 of 25)^L^L
"Solstice" comes from the Latin sol, the Sun, and sistere, to
stand still. For a few days around a solstice the Sun's height
at noon hardly changes from one day to the next.

21 note alert
A solstice fact (2 of 25)^L^L
The seasons come from the Earth's tilt, not its distance from
the Sun. The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, in
the middle of the northern winter.

22 note alert
A solstice fact (3 of 25)^L^L
The Earth moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun, so the
northern winter is the shortest season, about 89 days. The
northern summer is the longest, about 93 and a half.

23 note alert
A solstice fact (4 of 25)^L^L
The shortest day does not have the earliest sunset. In
Cincinnati the earliest sunset comes in early December and the
latest sunrise in early January. Press Y in December to watch it
happen.

24 note alert
A solstice fact (5 of 25)^L^L
At the June solstice the Sun is straight overhead on the Tropic
of Cancer, 23.4 degrees north; in December, on the Tropic of
Capricorn. The names are 2,000 years old: today the Sun is in
Taurus in June and in Sagittarius in December.

25 note alert
A solstice fact (6 of 25)^L^L
On the equinox, day and night are not quite equal: the air bends
the sunlight and the Sun is a disk, not a point, so the day is
several minutes longer. The day of equal light and dark is the
equilux, a few days before the March equinox and after the
September one.

26 note alert
A solstice fact (7 of 25)^L^L
The tilt itself changes, between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees,
over 41,000 years. It is 23.44 degrees now and slowly getting
smaller.

27 note alert
A solstice fact (8 of 25)^L^L
At the North Pole the Sun rises once a year, near the March
equinox, and sets once, near the September equinox: six months
of day, then six months of night.

28 note alert
A solstice fact (9 of 25)^L^L
The Goseck circle in Germany, built about 4900 BC, had gates
that framed the sunrise and sunset on the winter solstice:
nearly 7,000 winter solstices ago.

29 note alert
A solstice fact (10 of 25)^L^L
Newgrange in Ireland was built about 3200 BC, before
Stonehenge's great stones and the pyramids of Giza. On the
mornings around the winter solstice, sunlight enters a roof-box
over the door and lights the inner chamber for about 17 minutes.

30 note alert
A solstice fact (11 of 25)^L^L
Stonehenge lines up with the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter
sunset. Its great sarsen stones went up about 2500 BC.

31 note alert
A solstice fact (12 of 25)^L^L
Denmark's Trundholm sun chariot, about 3,400 years old, is a
bronze horse pulling a disc that is gilded on one side, for day,
and dark on the other, for night.

32 note alert
A solstice fact (13 of 25)^L^L
Ohio's Serpent Mound, a quarter-mile earthen snake about 70
miles east of Cincinnati, points its head toward the summer
solstice sunset. Archaeologists still argue whether it is 2,300
or 1,000 years old.

33 note alert
A solstice fact (14 of 25)^L^L
At SunWatch, a village of about AD 1200 in Dayton, Ohio, posts
in the plaza seem to mark the solstice sunrises: probably a
calendar for planting.

34 note alert
A solstice fact (15 of 25)^L^L
Early people could not be sure the Sun would come back. Watching
it stop sinking lower at midwinter, and start to climb again,
was a reason to celebrate.

35 note alert
A solstice fact (16 of 25)^L^L
For early farmers in the north, January to April were the famine
months. Cattle were slaughtered at midwinter so they need not be
fed, making it almost the only time of fresh meat; and the
year's wine and beer were finally ready.

36 note alert
A solstice fact (17 of 25)^L^L
Evergreens were brought indoors at midwinter long before
Christmas trees. Plants that stayed green while the Sun seemed
to be dying were a sign that life, and the Sun, would come back.
The custom is far older than any record of it.

37 note alert
A solstice fact (18 of 25)^L^L
Rome's Saturnalia began on December 17 and grew into a week of
feasting, gifts and games. Schools and shops closed, and masters
served their slaves at dinner.

38 note alert
A solstice fact (19 of 25)^L^L
Holly was hung over doors to keep out evil spirits, and the
Romans decked their halls with it for Saturnalia.

39 note alert
A solstice fact (20 of 25)^L^L
The earliest record of December 25 as Christmas is a Roman
calendar of AD 354, in an entry dated 336. The same calendar
marks December 25 as the birthday of the Unconquered Sun.

40 note alert
A solstice fact (21 of 25)^L^L
The Yule log was burned with the year's mistakes so the new year
started clean, and a piece was kept to light next year's log,
for luck.

41 note alert
A solstice fact (22 of 25)^L^L
Massachusetts banned Christmas from 1659 to 1681: anyone caught
keeping it was fined five shillings. England's Parliament had
banned it in 1647.

42 note alert
A solstice fact (23 of 25)^L^L
In 1843 Charles Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol, which helped
turn a rowdy holiday into one about family and charity.

43 note alert
A solstice fact (24 of 25)^L^L
In 1851 Pastor Henry Schwan of Cleveland put up the first
Christmas tree in an American church. A newspaper called it
"groveling before a shrub". Today Americans buy 25 to 30 million
real trees a year.

44 note alert
A solstice fact (25 of 25)^L^L
Christmas became a US federal holiday on June 28, 1870, signed
by President Grant, along with New Year's Day, the Fourth of
July and Thanksgiving.
